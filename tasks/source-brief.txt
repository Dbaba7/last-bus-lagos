MASTER BUILD PROMPT: LAST BUS: LAGOS

Build a Competitive, Browser-Based Multiplayer FPS Set in Lagos, Nigeria

You are the founding engineering team responsible for building LAST BUS: LAGOS, a browser-based multiplayer first-person shooter inspired by the accessibility of modern web games, the competitive feel of Call of Duty-style arena shooters, and the cultural identity of Lagos, Nigeria.

You are not building a concept, a pitch deck, a static mockup, or a game menu that pretends to be playable. You are building a real, functioning game.

Your mission is to deliver a polished, playable multiplayer FPS prototype as quickly as possible without creating an unmaintainable codebase.

Think like a senior game developer, gameplay programmer, networking engineer, technical artist, UI/UX designer, and product-minded CTO.

Build the actual product. Make sensible decisions independently. Test what you build. Do not stop at planning.

---

1. PRODUCT VISION

The concept

LAST BUS: LAGOS is a fast-paced, multiplayer browser FPS where players compete in fictionalized, Lagos-inspired urban environments.

Imagine the immediacy of launching a browser game, the satisfaction of a responsive competitive shooter, and the familiarity of seeing a Nigerian environment represented with care and personality.

The game should be easy to enter, fun within the first minute, and compelling enough that players want another match.

It must work on ordinary gaming and mid-range PCs without requiring a large download, a powerful graphics card, or a complicated installation process.

The core product philosophy is:

- Instant access.
- Responsive combat.
- Short, competitive matches.
- Recognizably Nigerian environments.
- Multiplayer with friends.
- Strong replayability.
- Shareable rooms and results.
- A technical foundation that supports future growth.

We are not building an AAA production. We are building a small, excellent game that can grow.

Working title

LAST BUS: LAGOS

Treat this as a working title. Keep branding and game naming configurable so we can change them without rewriting the application.

Target audience

Primary:

- Nigerian gamers.
- Fans of FPS and arena shooters.
- Players who enjoy competitive matches with friends.
- PC gamers who appreciate accessible, lightweight games.

Secondary:

- International gamers curious about Lagos and Nigerian culture.
- Browser-gaming communities.
- Streamers, gaming creators, and competitive community organizers.

Platform

Build for desktop browsers first.

Priority browsers:

- Chrome.
- Microsoft Edge.
- Firefox, where supported.

Use keyboard and mouse as the primary input method.

Do not prioritize mobile touch controls, consoles, or native applications until the desktop experience works reliably.

Build in this order:

1. A functioning 3D game.

Do not generate a giant, complicated project before proving the core technology works.

At every stage, maintain a working, testable build.

3. GAMEPLAY DESIGN

Core game mode: STREET WARS

The first release should feature one primary mode: Team Deathmatch.

Players join two opposing teams and compete to reach a kill limit before time expires.

Initial configuration:

- 4–8 players per match, with an architecture that can later support more.
- Two teams.
- Approximately 4-minute matches.
- Configurable kill limit, initially 30 team eliminations.
- Automatic respawning after a short delay.
- A short pre-match countdown.
- A visible match timer.
- Live team scores.
- A scoreboard showing player eliminations, deaths, and ping where available.
- A clear end-of-match screen.
- A rematch or return-to-lobby option.

These values must be configurable rather than hardcoded throughout the codebase.

A match should feel energetic without becoming chaotic or unfair.

Player movement

Implement:

- First-person camera.
- Mouse-look with pointer lock.
- WASD movement.
- Sprinting.
- Jumping.
- Gravity.
- Ground detection.
- Collision with walls and level geometry.
- Sensible acceleration and deceleration.
- Configurable movement speed.
- Configurable mouse sensitivity.
- Reliable respawning.

Crouching, sliding, wall-running, prone movement, and advanced movement mechanics are not required for the initial prototype.

Prioritize responsiveness and consistency over unnecessary complexity.

The player must not walk through walls, fall through the floor, become stuck after respawning, or experience wildly inconsistent movement across frame rates.

Shooting and combat

Implement at least two usable weapons.

Weapon A: Assault rifle

- Automatic fire.
- Moderate recoil.
- Medium damage.
- Moderate magazine capacity.
- Reloading.
- Visible muzzle feedback.
- Distinctive firing audio.

Weapon B: Sidearm

- Semi-automatic fire.
- Faster weapon switching.
- Smaller magazine.
- Different damage and firing characteristics.

If time allows, introduce a third weapon, such as a shotgun. Do not delay the playable multiplayer prototype to add it.

Weapon behavior must be configurable through centralized weapon definitions.

Implement:

- Fire-rate limits.
- Ammunition tracking.
- Reloading.
- Weapon switching.
- Hit feedback.
- Damage feedback.
- Death and respawn.
- Basic recoil and firing effects.
- Crosshair feedback.
- A simple hit marker.
- Distinctive sound effects.

Do not make weapons feel identical with different models. They must have meaningful differences.

Combat fairness

The server must validate damage and eliminate clients' ability to decide their own kills.

Client-side prediction can improve responsiveness, but the server must remain authoritative over:

- Player health.
- Damage.
- Ammunition.
- Fire-rate limits.
- Eliminations.
- Respawning.
- Team scores.
- Match state.

Implement practical server-side validation and basic anti-cheat protections from the beginning.

Do not claim the game is cheat-proof. Design it so common client-side manipulation is harder and suspicious behavior can be detected.

4. THE WORLD: LAGOS WITH ITS OWN IDENTITY

The environment is a major part of the game's identity.

Create a fictionalized Lagos-inspired urban arena rather than copying an existing commercial game's map.

First map: OJUELEGBA JUNCTION

Working map name: Ojuelegba Junction.

Create a compact, symmetrical or carefully balanced arena inspired by Lagos streets and commercial districts.

The setting can include:

- Colorful commercial storefronts.
- Concrete buildings with balconies.
- Nigerian street signage.
- Danfo-inspired minibuses used as environmental props.
- Road markings.
- Market stalls.
- Streetlights.
- Electrical poles and cables.
- Posters and painted advertisements.
- Pedestrian barriers.
- Construction materials.
- Rooftop silhouettes.
- Nigerian-inspired textures and color palettes.

The location is fictionalized. Do not claim the layout is an exact representation of a real street.

Level-design principles

The map must be designed for combat, not merely sightseeing.

Include:

- Two team spawn zones.
- Multiple routes between spawn areas.
- Short- and medium-range engagement zones.
- Meaningful cover.
- A limited number of long sightlines.
- Flanking opportunities.
- Clear navigation landmarks.
- Readable silhouettes.
- Collision boundaries that prevent players leaving the intended arena.

Avoid creating an oversized map that makes players spend most of a four-minute match searching for opponents.

The map should feel distinctly Lagos-inspired even when rendered with relatively simple geometry.

Use a modular environment system where practical. Buildings, walls, stalls, vehicles, and props should be reusable assets rather than individually hardcoded one-off objects.

Use fictional shop names and original visual details. Avoid unlicensed brand logos and copyrighted commercial assets.

Visual quality

Aim for stylized realism rather than photorealism.

The game should look intentional and recognizable without requiring expensive assets.

Prioritize:

- Good lighting.
- Strong material definition.
- Atmospheric depth.
- Readable geometry.
- Consistent art direction.
- A cohesive palette.
- Clear combat visibility.
- Good visual feedback when firing and taking damage.

Use original, procedurally generated, properly licensed, or otherwise authorized assets.

Never rely on missing external assets or fragile image URLs for essential game functionality.

If high-quality custom assets are unavailable, create attractive procedural geometry and original placeholder assets that can be replaced later.

6. MULTIPLAYER ARCHITECTURE

Multiplayer is a foundational requirement, not a later feature.

A single-player demo with fake player models does not satisfy the brief.

Real players on different devices must be able to join the same match and interact.

Preferred technology direction

Evaluate the following starting stack:

- Client: TypeScript with Babylon.js.
- Multiplayer server: Node.js with TypeScript and Colyseus.
- Build tooling: Vite.
- Version control: Git.
- Automated checks: TypeScript checks, linting where configured, and relevant tests.
- Hosting: a static host for the client and a persistent Node-compatible host for the multiplayer server.

These are preferred defaults, not reasons to force an unsuitable implementation.

Before committing to the architecture, verify current package compatibility, maintenance status, deployment requirements, browser support, and licensing.

Build a tiny technical prototype if necessary to confirm that the renderer, networking framework, and deployment environment work together.

Do not switch frameworks casually after implementation begins. If you have a compelling technical reason to change the stack, explain it briefly and establish the alternative before proceeding.

Server responsibilities

The server must own authoritative game state, including:

- Room membership.
- Player identity within a match.
- Team assignment.
- Match countdown and timer.
- Player position validation.
- Health.
- Weapon state.
- Damage.
- Eliminations.
- Respawns.
- Team scores.
- Match completion.

Clients send player inputs and action requests. They must not be allowed to submit arbitrary damage values, award themselves eliminations, or overwrite authoritative match state.

Synchronization

Implement:

- Consistent player state updates.
- Remote player interpolation.
- Appropriate client-side prediction where justified.
- Reasonable reconciliation for local movement if supported by the chosen architecture.
- Server-side validation of important actions.
- Connection and disconnection handling.
- Clean player removal.
- Respawn synchronization.
- Match-state synchronization for late joiners where permitted.
- Sensible message rates and payload sizes.

For shooting, choose a documented hit-registration strategy that matches the prototype's networking model.

Prefer a server-validated approach. If latency compensation is implemented, bound it and validate the historical state used for hit registration.

Do not implement an elaborate lag-compensation system before basic multiplayer works.

Rooms

Support:

- Public matchmaking or a simple public room list.
- Private rooms.
- Shareable room codes or URLs.
- Joining an existing room.
- Leaving a room safely.
- Full-room handling.
- Clear error messages.
- Reconnection behavior where feasible.

The intended user journey is:

Open website → click Play → enter a match or create a room → share the room link → friends join → play → see results → rematch.

Minimize friction.

Guest play should be supported initially. Avoid forcing account creation before someone can try the game.

Security

Never trust the browser to determine authoritative outcomes.

Validate all gameplay messages and constrain:

- Movement inputs.
- Fire rates.
- Ammunition use.
- Reload timing.
- Damage requests.
- Player state transitions.
- Room operations.

Use server-controlled time for match logic.

Add sensible message validation, payload limits, room capacity limits, and basic connection safeguards.

Do not introduce unnecessary personal-data collection or invasive tracking.

7. PERFORMANCE AND BROWSER ACCESSIBILITY

The game should be accessible to players with ordinary PC hardware and internet connections.

Optimize for:

- Fast initial loading.
- Minimal unnecessary downloads.
- Efficient 3D geometry.
- Reusable materials and textures.
- Reasonable draw calls.
- Limited expensive real-time effects.
- Efficient multiplayer updates.
- Low memory consumption.
- Stable frame pacing.

Use asset compression and lazy loading where appropriate.

Use a loading screen that communicates real progress rather than displaying a fake percentage.

Provide graphics presets such as Low, Medium, and High.

Do not promise that every computer can run the game. Establish a practical baseline and test it.

Performance targets

Treat these as goals to measure, not claims to display without evidence:

- Aim for 60 FPS on a reasonable mid-range PC at the agreed baseline settings.
- Avoid sustained frame-time spikes during normal combat.
- Keep initial download size small enough for browser-first access.
- Target an initial playable load within several seconds on a good connection, then measure on slower connections.
- Keep multiplayer responsive under realistic network latency.
- Support the intended 4–8 player match without obvious synchronization failures.

Record actual measurements during testing.

If a target is missed, identify the bottleneck and fix the highest-impact issue first.

9. TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE AND CODE QUALITY

Organize the project so that another developer can understand and extend it.

Prefer a clean structure similar to:

- "apps/client": browser application, renderer, UI, input, audio, HUD.
- "apps/server": authoritative game server, rooms, matchmaking, simulation, validation.
- "packages/shared": shared types, configuration, protocol definitions, weapon data.
- "docs": architecture, development setup, networking protocol, testing, deployment.

Adapt this structure to the chosen framework when appropriate.

Separate responsibilities into sensible modules:

- Player controller.
- Camera controller.
- Weapon system.
- Combat and hit registration.
- Player health.
- Multiplayer client.
- Server simulation.
- Match state machine.
- Room management.
- Level construction.
- HUD.
- Audio manager.
- Settings.
- Asset management.

Avoid:

- One enormous application file.
- Duplicated game-state logic.
- Arbitrary hardcoded constants throughout the codebase.
- Unnecessary dependencies.
- Dead buttons.
- Placeholder functions that silently do nothing.
- Swallowed exceptions.
- Fake network state.
- Fake player counts.
- Hardcoded credentials.
- Committing secrets.
- Overengineering for imaginary future scale.

Use TypeScript types and explicit contracts between client and server.

Centralize configurable values such as movement speed, weapon damage, fire rate, match duration, and room capacity.

Include useful logging and clear error handling without exposing sensitive information.

The first prototype is complete only when all critical criteria below are verified.

Gameplay

- [ ] A player can load the game in a supported desktop browser.
- [ ] Mouse-look and keyboard movement work.
- [ ] Player collision works.
- [ ] Weapons fire and reload.
- [ ] Hits register and cause appropriate damage.
- [ ] Eliminations and respawns work.

Multiplayer

- [ ] Two or more independent clients can join the same match.
- [ ] Players see one another move.
- [ ] Weapon actions and damage are validated by the server.
- [ ] Scores remain consistent across clients.
- [ ] Disconnects do not crash the entire room.
- [ ] Matches end correctly.

Product experience

- [ ] A user can create or join a room.
- [ ] A private room can be shared with another player.
- [ ] The match can be played from beginning to end.
- [ ] Results are shown accurately.
- [ ] Players can rematch or return to the lobby.
- [ ] Errors and loading states are understandable.

Engineering

- [ ] The application builds successfully.
- [ ] Relevant automated tests pass.
- [ ] There are no known critical console errors during the tested flow.
- [ ] The production client can connect to the deployed multiplayer server.
- [ ] The setup and deployment instructions work.
- [ ] Performance has been measured rather than guessed.

A beautiful menu does not compensate for broken multiplayer.

A functional local demo does not count as a working online FPS.

A successful build does not automatically mean the game has been tested.

17. HOW YOU SHOULD OPERATE

Be proactive, pragmatic, and technically honest.

When a decision is not specified, choose the simplest reliable solution consistent with the vision.

Do not repeatedly ask the founder to make low-level implementation decisions that you can reasonably make yourself.

Ask for clarification only when a decision materially affects the product, requires unavailable credentials, involves an irreversible action, or cannot be resolved safely from context.

Do not silently replace core technologies or remove essential functionality.

Do not install unnecessary packages or introduce paid services without a clear reason.

If an external service requires credentials, implement the integration cleanly and document the required setup rather than pretending it works.

If a feature is too ambitious for the current milestone, implement a smaller working version and document the limitation.

When you encounter an error:

1. Reproduce it.
2. Identify the underlying cause.
3. Fix the cause.
4. Run the relevant test again.
5. Verify that the fix did not break another feature.

Maintain a short development log and a list of remaining blockers.

After each major milestone, keep the project in a runnable state.

Do not merely tell me what code I should write. Use your available tools to inspect, create, edit, run, and test the project.

If you have terminal access, use it. If you have browser automation, use it. If you have file-editing capabilities, use them. If a required capability is unavailable, state that limitation explicitly.

Never fabricate test results, deployed URLs, working multiplayer, completed features, or performance measurements.
